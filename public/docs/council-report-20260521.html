LLM Council Report

Healthier — Value Proposition & Go-to-Market Strategy

5 advisors · 5 peer reviewers · 1 chairman synthesis  ·  May 21, 2026
Question brought to the council We're building Healthier — a health-services marketplace MVP in Buenos Aires connecting patients with healthcare professionals. We're about to test with 3 clínicas and expand by vertical weekly. Our hypothesis: start as SaaS for professionals (charge subscriptions while developing), then go for patient acquisition once the product is solid. Is SaaS-first the right wedge? What is the main value prop that will actually stick? What are the biggest risks?

The One Thing to Do First

In the three interviews, ask each professional to show you their last ten new patients — where those patients came from, how they found the professional, and whether any came through an online channel. That single data point will either validate or collapse the entire go-to-market hypothesis in under an hour, before you write a single line of code or collect a single peso.

The Recommendation

Do not launch a subscription before the three interviews. Use them to answer one question above all others: what do these professionals' patients look like, how do those patients currently find them, and would those patients have any reason to find them through a new platform instead?

The SaaS-first hypothesis is not wrong in principle, but as currently constructed, Healthier does not have tool-value yet. The SaaS wedge only works if there is a defensible answer to: "what does a professional get on day one, before a single patient books?" If the answer is only "a better profile page," that is not a subscription product.

The path that actually works: pick the single most acquisition-hungry professional from the three interviews, manually source 5–10 patients for them through WhatsApp groups, neighborhood networks, and direct outreach, and prove you can move patients to a professional before you build a billing system around the promise that you can. Once you've done that once — messily, by hand — you have validated the core marketplace mechanic. Then build the software that scales it. Then charge.

On regulatory risk: before spending 3–6 months building, get a 30-minute call with an Argentine health law attorney about colegio advertising rules and obras sociales compatibility. Not expensive. Could save you from building a product professionals can't legally use or patients can't practically access.

Where the Council Agrees

High-Confidence Signals (all 5 advisors converged)

  • The SaaS wedge is structurally broken as framed. Professionals are not paying for software — they are paying for patient flow. A Calendly embed and a profile page is not a subscription product; it is a prototype with a billing layer stapled on.
  • The sequencing is backwards. "Refine product first, acquire patients later" means you build for a hypothesis you cannot test. Patients will reshape what professional-facing features need to be.
  • The 30-day retention cliff is real. If a subscribed professional cannot point to a single booked patient within 30 days, the subscription dies — and word spreads negatively in the exact professional community you need to win.
  • The value prop that sticks is patient flow, not software features. Everything else — scheduling, analytics, "digital presencia" — is table stakes. Professionals will pay, renew, and evangelize one thing: "this platform sent me patients I wouldn't otherwise have reached."
Where the Council Clashes

Genuine Disagreement: When to Charge

One camp (Expansionist): Charge early. It establishes the professional relationship, funds development, and professionals who pay become advocates. Healthcare in Argentina runs on referral trust — one converted clínica unlocks a network of dozens.

Other camp (Contrarian + First Principles): Charging before you have switching cost creates expectation debt, not revenue. Professionals who pay and see nothing leave and talk. Don't charge until professionals would feel genuine loss at leaving.

The resolution: This is an empirical question, not a strategic one. It turns on whether there is a patient-independent reason professionals would pay right now (e.g., admin tooling, CRM, credentialing signal). The three interviews answer this. The Executor's advice is right: show a professional a price, ask for a credit card. Their reaction tells you everything.

Critical Blind Spot — Caught in Peer Review (5/5 reviewers flagged this)

Argentine healthcare infrastructure may not support a direct-to-consumer marketplace model.

Patient flow in Buenos Aires is predominantly mediated by obras sociales (union-based insurance, ~60% of formal workforce) and prepagas (OSDE, Swiss Medical, Medicus). Most patients don't search online and book independently — they ask their insurer which professionals are in-network, or get referred institutionally.

Additionally, the Colegio Médico and specialty colegios regulate how professionals can advertise and structure referral relationships. A subscription model where professionals pay for patient referrals could create legally ambiguous arrangements resembling fee-splitting under Argentine professional codes.

This isn't automatically fatal — but it needs to be on the agenda in the three interviews, not discovered six months into development. The question "are your patients accessible through an independent marketplace?" must be answered before you build.

Full Advisor Responses
The Contrarian — "The SaaS wedge collapses without patients"

Here's the fatal flaw nobody is saying out loud: professionals are paying for access to patients, not for software. If you charge them a subscription before patients exist on the platform, you're selling them a promise. The moment a professional asks "how many patients have booked through this?" and the answer is zero, the subscription dies. You haven't built SaaS — you've built a waiting room with a cover charge.

The "refine the product first, then acquire patients" sequencing is backwards. Professionals won't stay subscribed for 3-6 months hoping patients show up. Churn will hit before you've validated anything.

  • What are the 3 pilot professionals actually paying for today — before a single patient books?
  • What does their current patient acquisition look like? If they already have patients, why do they need you?
  • If they don't have patients, can your platform actually deliver them — and on what timeline?

The uncomfortable assumption: The team believes professionals will subsidize patient acquisition through subscriptions. But why would a professional pay monthly to sit on an empty platform? Argentine MedTech buyers are sophisticated and cost-conscious. "Pay now, patients later" is a very hard sell.

If you can't show a professional their first booked patient within 30 days of subscribing, retention collapses and word-of-mouth in the professional community turns negative — exactly the community you need to win.

The First Principles Thinker — "What are you actually trying to solve?"

Not "how do we monetize" — that's a constraint masquerading as a strategy. The real problem: How do you create irreversible momentum in a two-sided market with zero budget and limited time?

Strip the assumption that "SaaS-first" is a coherent wedge. SaaS means professionals pay for a tool. But what tool? Right now you have a Calendly embed. That's not SaaS — that's a wrapper around someone else's product. Professionals won't pay for that.

The first-principles question: what does a professional lose if they stop using Healthier tomorrow? If the answer is "nothing" — you don't have a product yet, you have a prototype. Charging for it creates a liability, not revenue. You're collecting money while creating expectation debt you can't service.

In a marketplace, the side with scarcity controls the dynamic. In Buenos Aires health services, patients are scarce (dispersed, habit-driven, trust-gated) and professionals are abundant. Professionals know this. They won't pay subscription fees for access to zero patients.

Don't charge yet. Get 5-10 professionals so locked into patient flow that removing them from the platform would hurt their practice. Then you have pricing power. The question isn't "SaaS or marketplace-first." It's "what creates switching cost?"

The Expansionist — "SaaS-first is an underrated launchpad"

Charging professionals early isn't just revenue — it's a data moat. Every professional who pays creates structured data about their specialty, availability, and patient volume. By month 12, Healthier could have the richest dataset on healthcare utilization patterns in Buenos Aires — valuable to insurers, pharma, public health agencies, and private clinics.

The bigger miss: professionals are the distribution channel, not just the customer. Healthcare in Argentina runs on referral trust. One converted clínica isn't one paying customer — it's a door into a trust network of dozens of affiliated professionals.

On value proposition: the thing that will actually stick isn't scheduling. It's status and patient quality. Professionals in Buenos Aires compete on perception. If Healthier makes a professional look more credible, more modern, more accessible — that's worth paying for. Position it as "your digital presencia," not "a booking tool."

The moonshot: Healthier becomes the de facto professional identity layer for Argentine healthcare — the way LinkedIn became career infrastructure. Network effects between professional reputation and patient reviews create a defensible moat no single-sided SaaS competitor can touch.

The Outsider — "Test the payment, not the product"

SaaS and marketplace are fundamentally different businesses with different success metrics, different sales motions, and different product priorities. "We'll be SaaS temporarily" doesn't exist — once professionals pay a subscription, they expect SaaS-level value. Calendly embeds and profile pages won't cut it.

You're asking professionals to pay before there are patients. That's not a marketplace — that's a directory.

"Build the product first, acquire patients later" assumes the product you build without patients is the right product for patients. That's almost certainly wrong. Patient behavior will reshape your professional-facing features significantly.

Why is patient acquisition being treated as a phase-2 problem? In Buenos Aires specifically, acquiring even 50 engaged patients costs almost nothing — word of mouth, WhatsApp groups, neighborhood Facebook pages, local gym partnerships. The marginal cost of a small patient cohort is near zero compared to the cost of building the wrong features for 3-6 months.

Have you asked professionals what they'd pay for? Not hypothetically — shown them a price and asked for a credit card? My outside read is that professionals in Argentina will agree to "yes interesting" and then not pay. Test the payment, not the product.

The Executor — "Solve this week's problem, not next quarter's"

You have 3 professionals lined up for interviews. That's your only asset right now. Everything else is theory.

Monday morning task: Before those interviews, strip your pitch to one sentence: "We send you patients in [specific vertical] in Buenos Aires for $X/month." That's it. Not "marketplace," not "platform." Do they say yes to that sentence? If they say "but where are the patients?" you have a chicken-and-egg problem you cannot SaaS your way out of.

The fastest viable path: Pick ONE vertical from your 3 professionals — whoever seems most acquisition-hungry — and manually source 5-10 patients for them before charging anything. Use WhatsApp groups, Reddit Argentina, neighborhood Facebook groups. Do it by hand. Prove you can move patients to professionals. Then charge for that proof.

What kills you fast: Building subscription billing before proving you can deliver the core promise (patient flow). If your SaaS charge is for "software features" rather than "patient acquisition," professionals will churn after month 2 when they don't see ROI.

One concrete gate: Can you get a professional to pay you — even $50 USD — for a referral you manually arranged? Yes? Build the software around that. No? Don't build the subscription model yet.

Peer Review Highlights
Peer Review Round — 5/5 agreement on strongest + blind spot

Strongest response (5/5 votes): The Contrarian (Response B)
Reviewers cited its precision in naming the fatal flaw, the concrete 30-day retention benchmark, and the three hard questions it forced the team to answer. "Waiting room with a cover charge" was the single most resonant framing across all reviews.

Biggest blind spot (5/5 votes): The Expansionist (Response A)
Unanimously called out for applying Series B thinking to a pre-seed validation problem. "Data moat with 3 professionals and zero patients is a spreadsheet, not a moat." Reviewers noted it answered the wrong question brilliantly — useful for Year 3, not Week 3.

What all five responses missed (5/5 independent convergence):
The Argentine healthcare regulatory and insurance infrastructure. Every reviewer independently surfaced obras sociales, prepaga networks, colegio de médicos rules, and the possibility that the team's target patients may not be addressable through a private marketplace. This is the single most important risk the council identified and the only one that could invalidate the entire go-to-market before a subscription is ever sold.
